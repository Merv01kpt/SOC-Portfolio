# Blue Team Foundations

## Overview

This repository documents a collection of foundational Blue Team security exercises completed through practical cybersecurity training. The modules focused on understanding the responsibilities of a modern Security Operations Centre (SOC), identifying how attackers exploit both people and systems, and applying defensive thinking to reduce organisational risk.

Rather than focusing solely on identifying technical vulnerabilities, these exercises emphasised the importance of structured security operations, incident response, user awareness, endpoint protection, and the collaboration required between multiple cybersecurity disciplines to effectively defend an organisation.

The practical scenarios reinforced that cybersecurity is not only about technology. Successful defence depends equally on skilled security professionals, secure organisational processes, informed users, and well-configured systems working together to minimise risk.

# Module 1 – SOC Role in Blue Team

## Introduction

The first module introduced the structure and responsibilities of a modern Security Operations Centre (SOC). Rather than viewing cybersecurity as a single job role, the training demonstrated how multiple specialised professionals work together to detect, investigate, contain and remediate security incidents.

A SOC operates as the central defensive capability of an organisation, continuously monitoring systems, analysing alerts, responding to threats and improving the organisation's overall security posture.

The module highlighted that effective cybersecurity depends on collaboration between multiple disciplines rather than isolated technical expertise.

---

## Blue Team Roles

Throughout the module, several key Blue Team roles were introduced.

### SOC Analyst

The SOC Analyst acts as the first line of defence by continuously monitoring security alerts generated by monitoring platforms such as Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) systems.

Responsibilities include:

- Monitoring alerts
- Performing alert triage
- Investigating suspicious activity
- Escalating confirmed incidents
- Documenting investigations
- Supporting incident response activities

This role reinforced the importance of analytical thinking and evidence-based decision making when determining whether security alerts represent legitimate threats or false positives.

---

### SOC Engineer

The SOC Engineer is responsible for designing, deploying and maintaining the tools that security analysts depend on every day.

Typical responsibilities include:

- SIEM administration
- Log collection
- Detection rule development
- Automation
- Security monitoring improvements
- Platform maintenance

This role demonstrated that effective detection depends upon properly configured security infrastructure.

---

### Threat Researcher

Threat Researchers analyse emerging cyber threats and identify attacker tactics, techniques and procedures (TTPs).

Responsibilities include:

- Threat intelligence research
- Monitoring emerging attack trends
- Producing threat reports
- Improving organisational awareness
- Supporting detection improvements

Understanding adversary behaviour enables organisations to strengthen defensive capabilities before attacks occur.

---

### Penetration Tester

Although penetration testing is traditionally associated with Red Team activities, the module highlighted its importance within an organisation's overall security strategy.

Penetration testers proactively identify weaknesses before malicious attackers can exploit them.

Their assessments assist Blue Teams by:

- Identifying security weaknesses
- Validating defensive controls
- Improving security architecture
- Supporting remediation efforts

---

### CERT Lead

The Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT) Lead coordinates the technical response during major security incidents.

Responsibilities include:

- Coordinating incident response
- Managing containment activities
- Organising communication
- Assigning technical responsibilities
- Supporting recovery operations

This demonstrated the importance of leadership and structured communication during cybersecurity incidents.

---

### Governance, Risk and Compliance (GRC)

The Governance, Risk and Compliance function ensures that security controls align with organisational policies, legal obligations and recognised security standards.

Responsibilities include:

- Security governance
- Risk assessments
- Compliance auditing
- Policy development
- Security awareness initiatives
- Continuous improvement

The module reinforced that effective cybersecurity extends beyond technology and requires organisational governance.

---

## Key Learning Outcomes

Completing this module strengthened my understanding that a Security Operations Centre functions as an integrated team rather than a collection of independent specialists.

Each role contributes unique expertise while supporting the shared objective of protecting organisational assets, reducing cyber risk and maintaining business continuity.

The module also reinforced the importance of communication, collaboration and structured incident management throughout the security lifecycle.

# Module 2 – Humans as Attack Vectors

## Introduction

People remain one of the most frequently targeted attack vectors in cybersecurity. Rather than exploiting software vulnerabilities alone, attackers often manipulate human behaviour through social engineering, deception and psychological techniques to bypass technical security controls.

This module explored how poor security decisions, insufficient awareness and unsafe user behaviour can unintentionally expose organisations to cyber threats.

Through realistic scenarios, the training demonstrated that even well-secured technical environments remain vulnerable when users are not adequately educated or when security policies are ignored.

---

## Practical Scenario – Suspicious Software Installation

One scenario involved a user downloading software from an unofficial source after failing to locate it on the vendor's official website.

Although antivirus software repeatedly detected malicious behaviour and prevented execution, the user continued attempting to run the application.

This exercise reinforced several important security principles.

Firstly, endpoint protection provides an important defensive layer but should never replace user awareness.

Secondly, repeated attempts to bypass security warnings significantly increase organisational risk and may indicate unsafe security behaviour requiring additional investigation.

Finally, downloading software exclusively from trusted and verified sources remains one of the most effective methods of reducing malware infections within an organisation.

---

## Human-Centred Security Risks

The module demonstrated that attackers commonly exploit predictable human behaviours, including:

- Curiosity
- Trust
- Urgency
- Fear
- Convenience
- Lack of security awareness

These behavioural characteristics are regularly abused during phishing campaigns, social engineering attacks, fraudulent communications and malware distribution campaigns.

Technical controls alone cannot eliminate these risks without ongoing user education and organisational awareness programmes.

---

## Security Awareness and Organisational Culture

An important takeaway from this module was that cybersecurity is not solely a technical responsibility. Every employee contributes to an organisation's overall security posture through the decisions they make each day.

Effective security awareness programmes help employees recognise suspicious activity, understand organisational policies and respond appropriately to potential threats before they develop into security incidents.

Developing a strong security culture significantly reduces the likelihood of successful phishing attacks, malware infections and accidental data exposure.

# Module 3 – Systems as Attack Vectors

## Introduction

While human behaviour is frequently exploited by attackers, insecure systems provide equally valuable opportunities for cybercriminals. This module focused on understanding how weaknesses in operating systems, software, configurations and endpoint security can be leveraged to compromise organisational assets.

The practical exercises reinforced the importance of proactive system maintenance, vulnerability management and defence-in-depth strategies to minimise exposure to cyber threats.

---

## Understanding System Vulnerabilities

The module introduced several common weaknesses that attackers routinely target, including:

- Outdated software
- Unpatched operating systems
- Weak system configurations
- Insecure default settings
- Unnecessary services
- Poor endpoint protection
- Weak authentication controls
- Misconfigured applications

Each of these weaknesses increases the potential attack surface available to malicious actors.

The training demonstrated that maintaining secure systems requires continuous monitoring, timely patch management and adherence to recognised security best practices.

---

## Defensive Security Principles

Throughout the exercises, emphasis was placed on implementing layered defensive controls rather than relying on a single security solution.

Important defensive measures included:

- Regular software updates
- Operating system patch management
- Endpoint detection and protection
- Secure configuration management
- Least privilege implementation
- Strong authentication practices
- Continuous monitoring
- Routine vulnerability assessments

These controls collectively reduce organisational risk and improve resilience against common attack techniques.

---

## Blue Team Perspective

One of the most valuable lessons from this module was learning to think from a defender's perspective.

Rather than asking how a system could be compromised, the exercises encouraged consideration of:

- How vulnerabilities are identified.
- How attacks are detected.
- Which controls reduce risk.
- How incidents are investigated.
- How systems can be hardened against future attacks.

This defensive mindset is fundamental to Security Operations Centre (SOC) environments where continuous improvement forms part of daily operations.

# Blue Team Workflow

Completing these three modules demonstrated how different areas of cybersecurity combine to support effective organisational defence.

A simplified Blue Team workflow includes:

1. Monitor systems for suspicious activity.
2. Identify potential threats.
3. Investigate alerts using available evidence.
4. Validate whether activity is malicious or benign.
5. Contain confirmed threats.
6. Eradicate malicious activity.
7. Recover affected systems.
8. Document findings.
9. Recommend improvements to reduce future risk.

Understanding this workflow reinforced that successful incident response relies on structured processes rather than isolated technical actions.
